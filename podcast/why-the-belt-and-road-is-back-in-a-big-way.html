Hello and welcome to another edition of the China Global South podcast, a proud member of the Sinica podcast network. I’m Eric Olander. Today, we’re going to get an update on the state of the Belt and Road Initiative.

Now, for the past several years, we’ve been hearing that the BRI is spent. The Chinese have run out of money and Global South countries that were the destination for so much of that investment simply can’t afford to take on more debt. And even the Chinese themselves have tried to change the narrative to make way for what was supposed to be a new, more austere era. Remember all of that talk about small yet beautiful? In Chinese, it’s called Xiao Er Mei (小而美). That was the line that they told everyone about smaller, more affordable, less risky BRI projects around the world.

Well, the data tells a very different story. BRI engagements last year actually reached an all-time high of more than $200 billion. Construction projects increased by 81% and investments surged by 61% compared to 2024. Energy engagements, especially in the fossil fuel sector, were very, very hot in 2025. And while the U.S. may have soured on Africa, Chinese investors haven’t. The continent was the top destination for BRI engagements anywhere in the world last year.

All of this comes from a new report published by the Green Belt and Road Center at Fudan University in Shanghai and Griffith University in Australia. Our old friend, Christophe Nedepil, is the man in charge of the project and joins us today from his office at Griffith University.

Questions and answers
Despite widespread belief that the BRI was 'spent,' what was the actual state of the Belt and Road Initiative in 2025 according to the new report?
Contrary to the narrative that the BRI was spent, engagements in 2025 reached an all-time high of more than $200 billion. Construction projects increased by 81% and investments surged by 61% compared to 2024.
The Chinese government previously promoted a 'small yet beautiful' (Xiao Armei) narrative for BRI projects. What did the 2025 data reveal about the scale and nature of these projects?
The data revealed a very different story, with BRI engagements reaching an all-time high. Instead of smaller, more affordable projects, the year saw massive deals, with some single engagements exceeding $10 billion, such as the $20 billion gas industrial park in Nigeria and a $10+ billion mining engagement in Kazakhstan.
Which specific sector experienced the most significant surge in BRI engagement in 2025, and what was its overall value?
Energy engagements, especially in the fossil fuel sector, were very active in 2025, reaching over $90 billion. This amount was more than double the energy engagement seen in the previous year and the highest since the BRI's inception.
How can 2025 be characterized as both the 'dirtiest' and 'greenest' year in terms of energy engagement within the Belt and Road Initiative, particularly considering the push for a 'green Belt and Road'?
2025 was the 'dirtiest' because fossil fuel engagement, particularly in oil and gas, constituted about 75% of the total energy engagement, representing a high-emitting sector. Simultaneously, it was the 'greenest' year because green energy engagement, including solar, wind, and battery storage projects, also increased to record levels in absolute terms.
Why have Chinese construction companies shifted their focus from public infrastructure like roads to more revenue-generating projects such as those in the fossil fuel sector?
This shift is driven by risk management and the necessity for projects to generate revenue. Fossil fuel projects are well-financed because the end product generates revenue, ensuring that construction companies are paid back. This is in contrast to public infrastructure projects, which often lack a strong revenue model and carry higher risks, especially given the debt sustainability issues faced by many Global South countries.
What role do Chinese private companies, especially in tech and green tech, play in BRI investments, and how does their involvement differ from state-owned enterprises?
Private companies, particularly new tech companies like TikTok, Alibaba, and green tech firms such as Jinko Solar, are leading the investment side of the BRI. They are motivated by being closer to customers, diversifying and de-risking supply chains, and leveraging their technological leadership. In contrast, state-owned enterprises are still heavily involved in construction engagements, which bring in direct revenue, but their ability to make overseas investments with their own money has changed due to different mandates and approval processes.
Africa was the top destination for BRI engagement in 2025 with a 283% increase. How might U.S. tariff policies and trade agreements like AGOA be influencing this surge in Chinese investment on the continent?
The surge in Chinese engagement in Africa is potentially linked to lower U.S. tariffs that African countries traditionally receive through the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA). Chinese companies making investment decisions look for favorable tax or tariff regimes to export competitively to the U.S. Africa, with its potentially lower tariffs, presents an opportunity for Chinese firms to set up manufacturing to bypass tariffs imposed on direct exports from China, although the renewed AGOA might only revert to 'Liberation Day tariffs' rather than zero tariffs.
Looking forward to 2026, what are some of the key trends expected to continue in BRI engagement regarding specific sectors and regional focuses?
For 2026, trends expected to continue include even more tech-related engagement, particularly in emerging economies, and increased activity in the mining sector, especially for transition minerals. Regionally, the Middle East is expected to maintain its consistent strong engagement across various sectors like energy, manufacturing, and real estate, while engagement in Africa and Southeast Asia is likely to fluctuate.
Why is the Middle East expected to remain a consistently strong region for BRI engagement, unlike the fluctuating interest seen in regions like Africa and Southeast Asia?
The Middle East has shown consistent strong engagement over the last years, encompassing a wide range of sectors including energy, manufacturing, and real estate. This sustained interest includes countries often overlooked by Western engagement, such as Iraq and Afghanistan, suggesting a deep and diversified commitment that makes it a stable focus for BRI activities.
