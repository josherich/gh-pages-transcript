Questions and answers
Why do young people associate Huang Xuan, and by extension the character Liu Feng in "Youth," with Wang Hongwen, a member of the "Gang of Four"?
The speaker believes young people are "frustrated," "helpless," "despairing" of reality, and seeking a "spiritual rebellion." They project a "rebel" image onto Liu Feng and Wang Hongwen, seeing Liu Feng as someone who "should have rebelled but didn't." This allows them to feel a sense of "utopia" and reflects their "despair about current reality."
As the author and screenwriter of "Youth," what was Yan Geling's reaction to the Bilibili video interpreting her work by linking Liu Feng to Wang Hongwen?
She had only watched one and a half episodes and found it "off-topic to the extreme" and "absurd." She stated that given her personal hatred for the Cultural Revolution, she would not use such an allegory in her novels.
What was Yan Geling's original intention behind creating the character Liu Feng, and was he ever conceived to be related to Wang Hongwen?
Liu Feng was based on a real "good person" (老好人) in the cultural troupe who was selfless and always helpful to others. Yan Geling explicitly stated that Wang Hongwen had no relation to Liu Feng, indicating the character was not conceived with such a connection.
How did the author, Yan Geling, approach the concept of class or social stratification when writing "Youth," and what was the reality of social stratification during the Mao era?
Yan Geling distinguished between "class" (a political concept) and "social stratum" (an economic/social concept), focusing on the latter through characters like He Xiaoping, who represented victims of political movements. The interviewer added that social stratification was arguably more rigid in the Mao era than during subsequent market economic reforms.
What is the significant difference between young people's understanding of the Mao era, particularly regarding "iron rice bowl" and "equal poverty," and the actual reality of that period?
Young people are drawn to the Mao era's promises of guaranteed employment and basic living standards, but the reality was widespread poverty, extreme scarcity of goods, and constant queuing for necessities, characterized as "equally poor." There were also hidden privileges for high-ranking cadres.
Why are young people increasingly adopting the "1644 historical view," considering the Ming-Qing transition as a "fracture of Chinese civilization," and how does this relate to interpretations of "Dream of the Red Chamber"?
Young people view the Yuan and Qing dynasties as foreign invaders and use this historical perspective to critique the present, often interpreting "Dream of the Red Chamber" through the "Red Chamber Mystification School." Yan Geling considers this "ridiculous" as Manchus are assimilated, and official narratives suppress this view because the Qing significantly expanded China's territory.
How has the "hatred education" since 1949 and subsequent political suppression contributed to the "breakdown" in historical understanding for younger generations, particularly regarding the Cultural Revolution?
"Hatred education" fostered mutual suspicion and persecution, peaking during the Cultural Revolution. After 1989, critical reflection and discussion of the Cultural Revolution were suppressed, leading to a "cut off" of historical truth by "manipulative people" and the CCP's need to maintain its legitimacy. This prevented a potential "Chinese Renaissance."
How has the tightening censorship environment impacted Chinese writers and their ability to create works that genuinely reflect the nation's experiences, and what are the consequences for Chinese literature?
Yan Geling describes increasingly severe censorship, especially after the pandemic, resulting in extensive cuts to her novels. She writes in English to avoid self-censorship and direct censorship, noting that this environment leads to a "great waste and harm" to talented Chinese writers, hindering the creation of "masterpieces" that could emerge from the nation's suffering.
