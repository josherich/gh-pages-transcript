  • Independent verifiability
  • Risky and narrow predictions with details that are hard to vary both before and after the fact.

Yeah, this is a very deep point. And I think that a lot of times we just define something with another definition, or we just throw out a piece of jargon as if that means we know something.

And, you know, it’s a difference between memorization and understanding. Understanding is a… You want to be able to describe it in 10 different ways in simple sentences from the ground up and re-derive whatever you need. If you’ve just memorized, you’re lost.

We’re collapsing them into one thing, like we do with electricity and magnetism or statistical mechanics and heat and thermodynamics. Those turn out to be one thing. We are attempting to do the same currently with gravity and quantum mechanics. We’re trying to reconcile them in quantum loop gravity or in string theory, or what have you.

You just kind of have to understand a few of the basic things, and then you’ll see them recurring over and over again. For example, I study and read physics and computer science just for entertainment. There’s no practical application for me, but there’s this really interesting theory I was reading about called Solomonoff induction. It basically formalizes Occam’s razor, which states that the shortest answer is the correct one.

However, it’s more advanced than that. It says that if you want to figure out why something happened, you take all the different reasons why it could have happened— all the different theories— and then you weigh each of them based on how compressed they are, how short they are, and how likely they are. That gives you the probability that provides an explanation for why that theory happened.

Now, Feynman has a very similar and elegant concept in a completely different field where he basically says, if you want to understand why this particle took that path, why did this photon take that path? What you do is you look at all the possible paths that the photon could have taken and you sort of weigh them. You sum them probabilistically based on how likely that path was.

It’s actually the same observation; one is about photons traveling through space-time, and the other is about causality and why an event may have happened and what the theory is behind that. These are two completely different fields with two completely different characters thinking about different things.

Yet, these are very, very fundamental theories in their fields. Deep down, they are actually the same, which is fascinating. It just shows how much of what we look at as complexity actually emerges from a set of very simple rules. Understanding is the thing to strive for, not the words. Why does Naval Ravikant generally avoid appearing on other people's podcasts?
He generally doesn't do podcasts anymore for a couple of reasons: he doesn't believe in sequels (they usually suck, cost more, are long-winded, the person has an ego to defend, they repeat themselves, and it's hard to come up with equally good material quickly), although he made an exception for Tim Ferriss as the "OG".
According to Naval, how does "science with a capital S" differ from actual science?
Naval believes "science with a capital S" is dangerous and propaganda, often becoming like social science or macroeconomics where data is cherry-picked. Actual science, based on Karl Popper's philosophy, requires falsifiability, independent verifiability, risky predictions, and rigorous testing, not consensus or voting.
What does Naval mean by the difference between "knowing the name of something and knowing something," using the bird example?
He means the difference between memorization (knowing a definition or term) and true understanding (being able to describe it in multiple simple ways, re-derive it, and understand the underlying principles). The bird example illustrates this: knowing a bird is a "warbler" tells you about humans, but knowing its behavior, diet, predators, and how it flies tells you about the bird itself.
What does Naval consider the most important principle from his "How to get rich" tweet storm and why?
The most important principle is "You're not going to get rich, renting out your time. You must own equity, a piece of a business to gain your financial freedom." He says this is the most important because in modern life, you need to productize yourself into a business and own that business, or at least own a piece of a business (like stock, partnership, or options), as it's extremely hard to get wealthy otherwise.
Naval states he doesn't follow his own 'making money' principles anymore. Why not?
He isn't actively trying to make money anymore because he's already become the type of person who makes money and has set up long-term situations where money makes itself. He optimizes for independence and freedom rather than maximizing wealth, preferring to optimize for lazy, flexible work hours and answering to no one.
What practices does Naval use for self-examination instead of traditional meditation?
He lists three: reading philosophy (especially at night to reflect), trying to be aware of his thoughts (viewing them with skepticism like an outside observer), and sitting down meditation for 60 minutes where he just lets whatever happens happen without forcing anything.
Why does Naval consider cryptocurrencies one of the greatest inventions?
He believes they are interesting because technology works best in unregulated spaces, and cryptocurrencies are a digital frontier allowing for truly trustless transactions without needing state authority. They enable disconnecting wealth creation, storage, and protection from the state, which is liberating.
What are the three main risks Naval associates with crypto stablecoins?
The three main risks are fraud risk (the stablecoin isn't actually backed 1:1 by dollars as claimed), censorship risk (a trusted entity like Coinbase could freeze or seize the stablecoins at the government's request), and blow-up risk (if the collateral backing the stablecoin, like Bitcoin or Ethereum, moves too drastically, the peg could break and holders could lose money).
What does Naval believe all effective self-help boils down to?
He believes all truly effective self-help boils down to choosing long-term over short-term.
