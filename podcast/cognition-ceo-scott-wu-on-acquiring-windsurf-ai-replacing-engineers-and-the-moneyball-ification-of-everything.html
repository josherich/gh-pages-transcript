Scott Wu describes a "moneyballification of everything," where fields like poker and chess have evolved from relying on intuition to being dominated by mathematical optimization. What causes this shift, and what implications does it have for creativity in these domains?
Scott explains that as a space matures, the underlying "math" becomes clearer and the "right questions to ask" become known. In less mature spaces, sharp intuition and first-principles thinking are crucial, but with maturity, the skills selected shift towards mathematical analysis and optimizing towards an "optimum," potentially leading to less obvious creativity in the traditional sense, as seen in the "romantic style of play" in chess giving way to optimized moves.
Cognition's Devin operates on an "async experience" model, delegating tasks at a "ticket level" or "project level," distinct from synchronous IDE tools. How does this async model make Devin's productivity impact more measurable than other AI coding tools, and what kind of gains are observed?
The async model makes Devin's productivity more measurable because it handles tasks "end to end," allowing for a clearer assessment of engineering hours saved, such as a migration being completed by Devin and a human merely reviewing the pull request in minutes. This approach leads to reported gains of "8 to 15x" for many use cases, with Devin typically merging 30-40% of all pull requests in a successful organization.
Given the rapid advancements by AI labs (e.g., GPT-6, GPT-7), how does Cognition, as an application layer company, plan to avoid getting "run over" by increasingly powerful general models?
Scott argues that "software engineering in the real world is so messy," involving significant "contextual knowledge" and "industry details" not easily covered by general intelligence. While general intelligence improves, the challenge lies in defining the specific "benchmark" for real-world tasks, such as fixing a Grafana dashboard where a server is running the wrong package version, which requires specific intelligence and product delivery experience that labs alone might not possess.
Devin, an AI software engineer, is used internally at Cognition for non-software tasks like ordering DoorDash, Amazon packages, and negotiating flight refunds. Cognition acknowledges the challenge of sites blocking bot activity but suggests a future where access is delegated, allowing agents to act "on your behalf" and attaching "reputation" to their actions, similar to API key permissions but at a mass consumer scale. This would involve the agent holding onto API keys or secrets, and the user being responsible for its actions, as seen in Devin successfully chatting with an airline agent and using contract links to secure a flight refund.
Scott Wu predicts a significant shift in software engineering within two to four years, where engineers might stop using code as the main interface. What does he envision the role of a software engineer will be in this future, and how will traditional computer science education remain relevant?
In this future, software engineers will primarily instruct computers, making architectural and high-level decisions (e.g., "we need to make a new page here," "save this data this way") without directly looking at code, similar to how assembly is not used today. Scott believes there will be "way more software engineers, not fewer," and traditional computer science education will remain absolutely vital for teaching theoretical concepts and problem-solving with the computer as a tool, rather than just syntax or specific frameworks.
