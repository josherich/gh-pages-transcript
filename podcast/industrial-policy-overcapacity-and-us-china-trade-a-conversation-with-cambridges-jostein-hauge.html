What is the main focus of the Cynical Podcast hosted by Kaiser Guo?
The Cynical Podcast is a weekly discussion of current affairs in China, examining books, ideas, new research, intellectual currents, and cultural trends to better understand China's politics, foreign relations, economics, and society.
What definition does the host use for "the West"?
Kaiser Guo defines "the West" primarily as Western Europe, Great Britain, and its colonial offshoots.
According to the guest, Jostein Halga, what are the root causes of the mainstreaming of anti-China sentiment in the West?
Jostein Halga believes the root causes include a subconscious bias among people in wealthy countries who feel they should be on top, and the emergence of China as an economic competitor and threat.
Jostein Halga describes a New York Times article calling China the "worst trading partner"; what counter-argument does he offer based on development theory?
Countering the idea of China as the "worst trading partner," Jostein Halga points out that development theory teaches that countries aiming to develop economically need to focus on export-led development and growth.
Why does Jostein Halga believe the accusation of "industrial overcapacity," particularly in renewables, doesn't make sense in the current global context?
Regarding renewables, Jostein Halga feels it makes no sense to discuss industrial overcapacity because the world is in the midst of a climate catastrophe, implying more production of clean energy technology is needed, not less.
How does Jostein Halga view China's development of technological capabilities in relation to technology transfer and intellectual property?
He views technology transfer as essential for developing countries to catch up and sees China as having used clever mechanisms, like joint ventures with foreign companies, to facilitate this, contrasting with the current strong IP protection regime that favors wealthy corporations.
What historical example does Jostein Halga use to show that powerful nations have historically used policies they now discourage in developing countries?
Jostein Halga cites Alexander Hamilton, a fierce supporter of industrial policy in early America, and references the book "Bad Samaritans" by Ha-Joon Chang, which argues that rich countries used industrial policy but then "kicked away the ladder" by advocating free trade for others.
According to Jostein Halga, what aspects of China's industrial policy reflect elements seen in East Asian tiger economies like South Korea and Japan?
Elements of China's industrial policy, such as the use of state-owned enterprises, some local content requirements, and joint venture requirements, reflect practices seen in the industrialization of East Asian tiger economies like South Korea and Japan.
How does Jostein Halga argue that China's rise, despite critiques, has benefited Western-based corporations?
He argues that Western-based corporations have benefited tremendously from China's integration into the global economy, citing Apple as a prime example that relies heavily on China's supply chains and has generated significant profits there.
How does Jostein Halga assess the impact of the US trade war and restrictions on China's push for innovation and self-reliance?
He suggests that while China's drive for innovation predates the trade war, the aggressive trade policy from the US has likely reinforced China's focus on national security and self-reliance, potentially accelerating its timetable for domestic innovation.
