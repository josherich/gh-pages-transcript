Questions and answers
What does Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney mean by a 'rupture' in the international order, and what does he identify as its causes?
Carney describes a 'rupture, not a transition' in the international order, stating that the bargain of mutual benefit through integration no longer works. He attributes this to great powers weaponizing economic integration through tariffs, financial infrastructure coercion, and supply chain exploitation.
According to Kobus van Staden, what was more significant about Mark Carney's speech: the break in substance or the break in optics?
Kobus van Staden argues that the speech was 'a break in optics, I think, more than necessarily that bigger break in substance,' suggesting that the visual presentation and acknowledgement of the order's failure were more impactful than immediate policy changes.
How does Liu Zhongyuan (Zoe) describe China's three main approaches to the existing international rule-based system?
Zoe outlines three approaches: first, China tries to 'change from within' by reforming institutions like the WTO, IMF, World Bank, and UN, often with Global South support; second, it 'tries to work only with Global South' through platforms like BRICS, SCO, or AIIB; and third, China sometimes 'go it alone,' as seen with its cross-border interbank payment system (CIPS).
Why does Zoe argue that China is unlikely to completely overthrow the existing U.S.-led international order, despite its frustrations?
Zoe explains that China has 'benefited tremendously from the existing international rule-based order,' particularly after joining the WTO. She argues that 'if you destroy the system from which you benefit from, you do not know what is coming next, the opportunity cost is too huge.' While China feels constraints, it seeks to reform rather than abolish the system.
What does Zoe identify as the main reasons behind the persistent 'debt trap diplomacy' narrative surrounding China, despite empirical evidence debunking it?
Zoe attributes the narrative's pervasiveness to 'China's opaque lending practices and the political leverage that appears to be built into its financing model have led to global unease.' She also points to China's 'very, very bad PR' and a lack of understanding by Chinese actors of how other countries, particularly in the Global South, operate, often failing to consider regime change or tribal conflicts.
How does Zoe offer an 'underappreciated' perspective on the impact of cheap Chinese goods on Western economies, particularly in relation to inflation?
Zoe suggests that 'when Chinese goods were cheap, before tariffs, it also meant Western countries who were importing Chinese goods—these cheap goods actually could have the effect of smoothing out part of the inflationary pressure.' She notes this aspect is often overlooked, with economists focusing instead on the risk of China exporting deflation.
According to Zoe, what is the 'dual identity' challenge China faces in the international system, and why does it struggle to maintain it?
China struggles to maintain its 'dual identity' as both a global superpower (at least economically) and a self-proclaimed member and leader of the Global South. Zoe notes that this dual identity allows China to align with countries sharing grievances against former colonial powers or non-rule writers, helping it assert 'strategic autonomy' without engaging in Western discussions about democracy or human rights.
According to Eric, how do China's opaque business practices contribute to the credibility gap and the persistence of narratives like the 'debt trap'?
Eric argues that while external propaganda plays a role, 'the Chinese have done themselves no service whatsoever by the opacity that they conduct their business around the world.' He notes their absence from media and public conversations, which fuels 'rumors of debt traps, overcapacity, other things that they really resent to their core.'
